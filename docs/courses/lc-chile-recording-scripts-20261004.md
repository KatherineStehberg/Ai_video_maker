## RPM-2026-001 — Asynchronous Communication & Team Chat Etiquette

Curso: English for Remote Work B1–B2

Fuentes: https://gamma.app/docs/RPM-2026-001-VIDEO-SCRIPT-v01-Asynchronous-Communication-amp-Team-o027h1fc8i8rdej

### The problem

You send a message on Slack. Three hours later, the reply is: Which file? This lesson will help you write complete messages so a colleague can understand and act without unnecessary back and forth. Asynchronous communication means exchanging information without expecting an immediate reply. The goal is not to write more. It is to provide enough context the first time.


### A complete message

Use five parts: greeting, context, clear request, time expectation, and next step. Listen to this example. Hi Ana. Could you send me the Q3 report? I need it by three PM today for the budget presentation. Let me know if the timing is an issue. The reader knows which report, why it matters, when it is needed, and what to do if there is a problem.


### Professional tone

Directness depends on urgency, your relationship, hierarchy, and the situation. Send me the file may sound sharper than you intend. Could you please send me the file when you have a moment is more diplomatic. For account access, use the approved access process rather than asking someone to share a password. To request an update, say: Just checking in on the report. Could you share your estimated completion time today?


### Report a blocker

A blocker prevents work from moving forward. Report it early, name what you are waiting for, and give a revised estimated completion time. Here is a simulated example. I am currently waiting on the pricing information before I can complete the proposal. My revised ETA is tomorrow afternoon. I will update you when the figures arrive. This describes the problem and the next step without blaming a colleague.


### Useful vocabulary

ETA means estimated time of arrival; in a task update it often means when you expect to finish. EOD means end of day. State a time zone when it could be unclear. OOO means out of office. WFH means working from home. FYI means for your information. Keeping someone in the loop means keeping them informed. Use these expressions only when your audience understands them.


### Pause and practise

Pause the video. Rewrite this message: Hi. Need the report. Add a specific report, purpose, deadline, and next step. Use a fictional workplace situation. Read your message aloud. Can the recipient act without asking which report or when? Before sending, check the purpose, context, time expectation, tone, and any blocker. Complete the matching practice lesson in the course. You can repeat the exercise at your own pace.


## RPM-2026-002 — Remote Work Foundations

Curso: English for Remote Work B1–B2

Fuentes: https://gamma.app/docs/RPM-2026-002-VIDEO-SCRIPT-v01-Remote-Work-Foundations-ulqt5iowo85mn48

### Your first day

It is your first day working remotely for an international company. Your manager sends a video call link. How do you introduce yourself with confidence? In this lesson you will describe your role, responsibilities, tools, and routine, and ask useful onboarding questions.


### Remote vocabulary

Remote work means working outside a traditional office. A distributed team has members in different locations. Onboarding is the process of integrating a new employee. A check-in is a brief meeting or message to share updates. A deadline is when a task must be completed. A deliverable is the completed output you provide to a colleague, client, or manager.


### As, in, and for

Use work as for a job title: I work as a UX designer. Use work in for a field: I work in marketing. Use work for for an employer: I work for a remote-first startup. To describe responsibilities, say: I am responsible for generating weekly reports. To describe a routine, use: I usually start work at nine and review my tasks first.


### Model introduction

Listen to a fictional example. Hi everyone, nice to meet you. My name is Valentina Torres, and I am a data analyst at a fintech company based in Santiago. I work remotely with a distributed team. I am responsible for generating weekly performance reports and supporting the business intelligence team. I usually start my day by reviewing my tasks and joining a check-in with my manager. I am excited to be part of this project. Notice the sequence: name, role, company, responsibilities, routine, and positive closing.


### Onboarding questions

You do not need to understand everything immediately. Ask: Could you tell me more about my responsibilities? What tools does the team use? Who should I contact if I have a question? Close with: I am looking forward to working with the team. These questions help clarify expectations while keeping a professional tone.


### Pause and practise

Complete this sentence: I work blank a content writer blank a digital marketing agency. Pause before listening to the answer. The answer is: I work as a content writer for a digital marketing agency. Do not say I work in a project manager. Say I work as a project manager. Do not say responsible of. Say responsible for. With I, say I usually start, not I usually starts.


### Your profile

Write a short remote work profile with four parts: who you are, your responsibilities, your tools, and your daily routine. Use at least three structures from this lesson. Record yourself reading it, then check that the role and responsibilities are clear. Keep it with your course practice work. Continue through the curriculum at your own pace; no live attendance is required for this activity.


## RPM-2026-003 — AI & Productivity

Curso: English for Remote Work B1–B2

Fuentes: https://gamma.app/docs/RPM-2026-003-VIDEO-SCRIPT-v01-AI-Productivity-8qcv4udi2fgqn5c

Adaptación: Source-adapted narration: LMS references, individual practice, corrected ownership example, no automatic course completion/teacher review claims. Fictional examples labelled; unsupported ATS timing claims removed.


### Work with AI

You have five minutes to write a status update for an international team. Could AI help you prepare a draft while keeping the message accurate? This lesson introduces vocabulary and structures for describing AI tools, writing prompts, and reviewing results. You remain responsible for the final message.


### Key vocabulary

A prompt is an instruction you give to an AI tool. Productivity concerns the efficiency of work output. To automate is to make a task happen with less manual effort. A workflow is the sequence of steps used to complete a task. AI-assisted means supported by AI. A draft is a first version of a text, before final review.


### Describe the benefit

Listen to three useful patterns. I use ChatGPT to draft emails for my clients. It helps me save time on weekly reports. You can automate repetitive tasks like data entry. After I, use the base form: I use. With it, use helps. After can, use automate, not automation. Could, should, and might can help you make suggestions politely.


### Task, context, format

A strong prompt gives a task, context, and format. Write an email is too vague. A stronger instruction is: Write a short, polite email to a client explaining a two-day delivery delay. Keep it under one hundred words. The task is an email, the context is a delivery delay, and the format includes tone and length. Add only information you are allowed to share.


### A workplace update

For a simulated weekly status update, give the project name, current phase, progress, blockers, and next steps. Ask the tool to use a professional tone for an international audience. Then compare every claim with your notes. Correct dates, names, missing actions, and unsupported achievements. Ask for a revision if the output is too long or unclear. A fluent sentence is not evidence that its content is true.


### Pause and practise

Improve one weak prompt: Make a report, Fix this text, or Give me ideas. Pause and use this structure: Write a type of text for an audience, about a topic, in a specified tone and length. Use a fictional example rather than confidential company data. Read the prompt again. Would another person understand the requested task and output?


### Your challenge

Choose a meeting summary, follow-up message, or task checklist. Write a prompt in English, generate a draft if you have access to an approved tool, and document one improvement you made during review. Complete the AI and Productivity practice lesson and its knowledge check. Keep both the prompt and the revised text so you can see the difference between a generated draft and your final decision.


## RPM-2026-004 — Professional Communication

Curso: English for Remote Work B1–B2

### Communicate clearly

Imagine joining a video call late while your manager is presenting. You need to join politely and clarify what you missed. This lesson covers professional emails, meeting language, updates, and diplomatic feedback. Clear communication helps the reader or listener understand the next action.


### Meeting vocabulary

An agenda lists meeting topics. Minutes are a written summary of what was discussed. An action item is a task assigned after a meeting. A stakeholder has an interest in a project outcome. To clarify is to make something easier to understand. Diplomatic language is careful and polite. To join a discussion, try: Sorry to interrupt. Could you clarify the next action?


### Five-part email

Use a greeting, context, clear request, deadline, and polite closing. Listen to this model. Subject: Feedback on Q3 Report. Hi Carla, I hope you are doing well. I am writing to follow up on the Q3 report I shared last Friday. Would it be possible for you to review it before our meeting on Thursday? Thank you in advance for your time. Best regards, Diego. Could and would soften the request without hiding the action.


### Status update

In a simulated stand-up, the team lead asks: Ana, how is the client presentation coming along? Ana replies: It is going well. I finished the first draft yesterday. I am currently waiting on the final numbers from the finance team before I can complete the last slide. My revised ETA is Wednesday. The answer covers progress, the blocker, and the revised completion time.


### Diplomatic feedback

Instead of saying This report has too many mistakes, try: I noticed a few areas we could refine. Would you like to go through them together? Instead of That is not a good idea, try: I see your point. I would like to suggest another approach we could consider. Keep feedback specific and useful. Acknowledge what works, explain the improvement, and invite the next step.


### Pause and practise

Rewrite: You did not attach the file. Pause the video and describe the problem without an accusation. One possible version is: I could not find the attachment. Could you please resend it? Now add a deadline if the task requires one. Check that your request stays clear; being polite does not mean removing the action or urgency.


### Choose a challenge

Write five or six sentences requesting feedback, using the five-part email structure. Alternatively, record a one-minute spoken update: what you completed, what you are working on, and one blocker with a revised ETA. Listen once and revise one sentence. Complete the corresponding practice lesson. Save a phrase you can reuse in a future email or meeting.


## RPM-2026-005 — Remote Collaboration

Curso: English for Remote Work B1–B2

Fuentes: https://gamma.app/docs/RPM-2026-005-VIDEO-SCRIPT-v01-Remote-Collaboration-6ucegtm0wdvh7oq

Adaptación: Source-adapted narration: LMS references, individual practice, corrected ownership example, no automatic course completion/teacher review claims. Fictional examples labelled; unsupported ATS timing claims removed.


### Across time zones

Imagine a team with members in Santiago, Lisbon, and Manila. You have limited daily overlap and a client deliverable due on Friday. How do you keep work moving when everyone is not online together? This lesson focuses on ownership, channel choice, blockers, and clear hand-off notes.


### Four pillars

Remote collaboration needs clarity, the right channel, accountability, and respectful communication. State who does what and by when. Use asynchronous communication for updates and documentation. For a complex or sensitive issue, consider a conversation where people can ask questions immediately. Follow up on your commitments and describe problems without blaming colleagues.


### Useful task vocabulary

A sprint is a short, fixed period of focused work. A backlog is a list of tasks waiting to be worked on. A stand-up is a brief check-in. An owner is the person responsible for a task. A dependency is something that must be completed before another task can proceed. A hand-off passes work to another person so they can continue.


### Confirm ownership

Listen to this consistent exchange. Could you take ownership of the client email? I will handle the report. The colleague replies: Got it. I own the client email, and you own the report. Is that correct? Repeating the allocation prevents confusion. For a blocker, say: I am currently waiting on the sales figures before I can complete the report. Describe what you need and who can help.


### A complete blocker message

Here is a fictional example. Hi team, I am blocked on the client report because I am waiting for the sales figures. Tomas, could you follow up with the sales team? I would like to finish by end of day if possible. Tomas replies: I will follow up now and update you within the hour. The task, blocker, request, and timeframe are all explicit.


### Pause and practise

Would you discuss a sensitive disagreement about a client project in a long chat thread or in a scheduled conversation? Pause and consider complexity, sensitivity, and who needs to participate. Explain your choice. Then rewrite Can someone look at this? Name the task, owner, and deadline so a colleague can act.


### Your hand-off

Write a hand-off note covering progress, pending work, blockers, and where the information is stored. Start with: Hi Camila, handing this off to you. Here is where things stand. Include a next action and a deadline with a time zone if necessary. Read the note as if you were the next person starting work. Complete the Remote Collaboration practice lesson and keep this note for your final project.


## RPM-2026-008 — The International Resume & Action-Oriented CV

Curso: English for Remote Work B1–B2

### Relevant evidence

Your resume should help a reader understand your experience and contribution quickly. This lesson focuses on relevant sections, simple formatting, truthful keywords, and action-oriented experience statements. Requirements vary by country and employer, so adapt your document to the target role.


### Sections and privacy

A useful structure is contact information, professional summary, key skills, work experience, education, certifications, and relevant links. Include your name, city and country, professional email, and an appropriate contact number. Add a portfolio or LinkedIn profile when relevant. Avoid unnecessary personal details such as national ID, date of birth, marital status, or full home address unless the specific application requires them.


### ATS and summary

An applicant tracking system, or ATS, helps organizations manage applications. Use standard headings, readable text, and straightforward formatting. Match role keywords only when they reflect your real experience. Your summary should explain your role, scope of experience, strengths, and contribution. A generic statement about being hardworking gives the reader less evidence than a specific description of what you can do.


### Action, scope, value

Compare Responsible for social media with Managed weekly company updates and responded to technical inquiries. The second statement names the action and the work. Another example is: Coordinated weekly project updates across three teams, improving visibility of deadlines and delivery blockers. Use only the numbers and outcomes you can support. A clear qualitative contribution is better than an invented metric.


### Choose a verb

Leadership verbs include led, coordinated, managed, and facilitated. Improvement verbs include improved, streamlined, and reduced. Execution verbs include implemented, developed, and designed. Communication verbs include presented, documented, negotiated, and resolved. Use present tense for current responsibilities and past tense for completed work. Select the verb that accurately describes your contribution.


### Pause and practise

Improve Responsible for answering customer emails. Pause and write an action-focused sentence using real evidence. One model is: Resolved customer support tickets in English and documented recurring issues. Use this model only if it describes what you actually did. If you practise with a fictional profile, label it as a simulation and never submit the invented experience as your own.


### Build version one

Prepare a concise summary, relevant skills, and your actual experience with clear bullet points. Add education, genuine certifications, and working links. Check spelling, verb tenses, readability, and every claim. Save version one with your practice work and read it aloud. This self-paced activity does not include an automatic promise of teacher review. Use the checklist to identify one improvement before your next revision.

### Lead a short meeting

A useful meeting has a clear purpose, an agenda, and agreed next steps. In this module you will practise opening, guiding, and closing a short meeting in English. You can simulate both the chair and a participant by recording two short speaking turns.


### Open with an agenda

Prepare three points: progress, blockers, and next steps. Open with: Thank you for joining. Today we will review progress, discuss blockers, and agree on next actions. Does anyone need to add an item? This tells participants why they are meeting and gives them a chance to clarify the agenda.


### Contribute politely

Use I would like to suggest to make a proposal. Use Could I add one point to enter the discussion. To clarify, ask: Could you explain what you mean by that? To confirm agreement, say: So we agree that the draft is due on Thursday. Is that correct? Give others time to answer instead of assuming silence means agreement.


### Worked simulation

Imagine a project update. The chair says: Let us start with progress. The participant replies: The first draft is complete, but the final figures are still pending. The chair asks: What do you need to move forward? The participant answers: Confirmation from finance by Wednesday. The chair concludes: Please follow up with finance today and update the team tomorrow. Notice the movement from progress to blocker to a named action.


### Close with ownership

At the end, summarize the action, owner, and deadline. For example: Sofia will contact finance today. Daniel will update the draft on Thursday. We will review the final version on Friday. Check whether the timing is realistic and specify the time zone when necessary. A meeting is easier to follow when decisions become concrete actions.


### Pause and practise

Write your own three-point agenda for a real or simulated work topic. Record an opening, one clarification question, and a closing summary. Play it back and check that a listener could identify the purpose, decision, owner, and deadline. Save the agenda and recording for the final project. Repeat with a different role if you want more speaking practice.

A professional email or executive report helps someone understand a situation and decide what to do next. This module focuses on structure, clarity, and tone. Start by identifying the reader, your purpose, and the action or information they need.


### Email structure

Use a specific subject, greeting, context, request, closing, and signature. A subject such as Q3 report: confirmation requested is clearer than Hello. Keep related information together. Compare this message: Hey, just wanted to check if you got my last email about the report. Let me know ASAP. ### Present one clear idea

A professional presentation needs an opening, a structured body, a closing, and a way to handle questions. Your practice task is a two-to-three-minute presentation about your area of work. Select one idea your audience should remember, rather than trying to explain everything.


### Opening and structure

Open with your topic and purpose. For example: Today I will explain our proposed reporting process and the next steps for the team. Then show the structure: First, I will describe the current situation. Next, I will explain the proposed change. Finally, I will summarize the actions. These signposts help the listener follow the argument.


### Develop the message

Use one main point at a time and support it with a clear example. In a fictional scenario, a team receives updates in several separate messages. A proposed improvement is a shared weekly summary. Explain the benefit cautiously: This could make deadlines easier to track. Do not claim measured time savings unless you have actually measured them.


### Close and invite questions

Summarize the idea and requested next action. Try: To summarize, we propose one weekly update with progress, blockers, and next steps. The next action is to test the format on one project. Thank you for your time. I am happy to answer questions. The closing connects the presentation to a decision or practical activity.


### Handle a question

Listen fully before answering. You can say: Thank you for the question. Could you clarify which part you mean? If you do not know, say: I do not have that information yet. I will check it. A clear admission is better than inventing an answer. For individual practice, write two likely audience questions and record your replies.


### Pause and record

Prepare your opening, two or three main points, and closing. Record a two-to-three-minute presentation, then listen once without reading your notes. Check structure, pace, transitions, and whether the requested action is clear. Revise one section and record it again. Save the improved version and your two question responses for your final project.

### Support a work task

AI can support drafting, summarizing, translating, and revising workplace text. In this module you will choose one task, write a clear instruction, review the result, and record what improved. This is a practice workflow, not a promise that every AI output will be accurate or save time.


### Choose safe material

Start with a fictional document or material you are authorized to use. Decide what the tool should produce and for whom. For example, you might need a short team update from meeting notes. Do not include private client, employee, or company information unless your approved process permits it.


### A structured prompt

Give a task, context, and format. Here is a model: Summarize these fictional meeting notes for an international operations team. Use five bullet points covering decisions, owners, and deadlines. Do not add facts that are missing from the notes. This instruction is more useful than Summarize this because it tells the tool what to prioritize.


### Review before using

Compare the output with the input. Check names, dates, figures, action owners, and any unsupported claim. If the result says the team approved a decision that was only discussed, correct it. If the tone is too formal or vague, ask for a specific revision. Keep responsibility for the final message instead of assuming polished language means reliable information.


### Describe the activity in English

Use these structures: I used an AI tool to prepare a first draft. I checked the dates against the original notes. I revised the message for our audience. The tool helped organize the information. If you measure time, include the time spent reviewing and correcting. Do not invent a productivity percentage.


### Pause and practise

### Work towards agreement

Negotiation language helps you propose, respond, make a concession, and confirm an agreement. In this module you will simulate a workplace conversation. You can record both sides individually. Choose a realistic topic such as a deadline, division of work, or meeting schedule.


### Make a proposal

Use I would like to suggest, What if we, or Our proposal is. For example: I would like to suggest delivering the first draft on Thursday and the final version on Monday. Give a reason so the other person can evaluate the proposal. Keep your request specific enough to discuss.


### Acknowledge and respond

To acknowledge a point, say: I understand your concern. To offer a concession, say: We could consider a shorter first draft. To explain a limit, say: We cannot confirm Monday until the figures are available. A courteous response can still communicate a clear boundary.


### Worked dialogue

Imagine two fictional teams discussing a report. Team A says: Our proposal is to deliver the final report on Friday. Team B replies: I understand your point, but we need time to check the figures. What if we share a preliminary summary on Friday and the checked report on Monday? Team A responds: That works for us, provided the summary clearly labels unconfirmed figures. Team B concludes: Agreed. We will send the summary Friday and the checked report Monday.


### Confirm the agreement

After agreeing, summarize scope, owner, deadline, and any condition. Use We agree on or Let us move forward with. Make sure both sides understand whether the deliverable is a draft or final version. Write a short confirmation message so the agreement does not depend on memory alone.


### Pause and roleplay

Write a proposal, one concern, a concession, and a final agreement for your scenario. Record both roles and listen back. Did you acknowledge the other position? Is the final action realistic and clear? Save a four-sentence agreement summary. Reflect on which phrase helped you respond politely without losing the purpose of the negotiation.
